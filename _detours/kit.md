For years I stumbled over the same few interview questions. Not because the answers were bad, but because I'd never said them out loud until the moment it counted. This kit is what I wish I'd had back then. Take what's useful and make it yours.

## 1. The gap question, in two sentences

When someone asks about a stretch on your timeline, the gap is rarely the problem. The nervous five-minute explanation is.

Use two sentences:

1. Say what happened, plainly.
2. Say what you were looking for next, and how the next role gave you that.

Here's mine: "Quibi shut down, and I stayed on through the wind-down into May 2021. After that I took a few months to reset and was picky about what came next, because I wanted to own the data side of talent work, and the Flowserve role was exactly that."

Then stop talking. Let them move on. If they want more, they'll ask.

## 2. Contract roles, without the defensiveness

A run of contract or consulting roles can look like drift on paper. Usually it isn't. Tell the throughline, not the list.

Try: "I followed the work more than the title. Each role gave me [the skill or problem], and that's what led me to [where you are now]."

Mine: "After five years at HBO I wanted to see how other companies ran talent, and consulting let me do that fast. Each role pulled me further from filling roles and closer to building the systems behind them."

Keep it warm and short, then bring the conversation back to what you'd do for them.

## 3. Practice out loud, not just in your head

Researching what to say and practicing saying it are two different things. Before your next interview or hard conversation:

1. Write your two-sentence answers for the questions you dread.
2. Say them out loud three times. Record yourself once and listen back.
3. Practice once with a person who will push back a little.

If you'd like a practice partner that pushes back, that's why I built CareerPaver: you can rehearse an interview before the real one.

## 4. My rules for using AI honestly

I build with AI every day, and I think how you use it matters as much as whether you use it. These are the rules I hold myself to:

1. **Say when it's AI.** The chat on my site is labeled as an AI version of me, right in its header.
2. **Never let it invent facts.** It can only use things I've confirmed. If it doesn't know, it says so.
3. **Every number needs a source you can click.** If I can't link it, I don't use it.
4. **I review it until I trust it.** Everything I automate started with me approving each piece by hand, and automated checks keep watching after that.

Use AI to help you prepare, not to pretend. A hiring manager will remember the person who was honest about how they got ready.

## One last thing

The detours in your career aren't a story you need to apologize for. They're usually the most interesting part of it, once you can tell them in two sentences.
